# Invasive Knotweed Spread Within UK Infrastructure

**A Corporate Information Statement from Advanced Invasives Ltd on how Invasive Knotweeds came to be present in UK rail, road and canal corridors, and on the mechanisms by which they continue to spread along them.**

Dr Daniel Jones, Dr Lois Child and Dr John Bailey · Issued 23 September 2026

[**Read the statement (PDF)**](invasive-knotweed-uk-infrastructure.pdf)

---

## Why this exists

Ask how Japanese Knotweed came to be on a railway embankment and you will usually be told that the railway companies planted it there to hold the soil together. The claim is repeated in site reports, in correspondence between advisers, in court, and in conversation among people who manage the assets concerned. So far as we are aware, it has never been tested against the documentary record in a published position statement.

This is that test. It sets out what the record supports, what it does not, and where the honest answer is that nobody knows.

The question is not merely historical. Where a Knotweed stand is found beside a railway, a road or a canal, an assumption about how it got there shapes liability, disclosure, the scope of a survey, and the design of a management regime. A history cannot be changed. A method can.

## The position in short

- **Spread is clonal, and disturbance drives it.** In the UK, Invasive Knotweeds spread almost entirely by vegetative dispersal rather than by seed, and both anthropogenic and natural disturbance encourage that spread (Jones et al. 2018).

- **The invasive clone arrived after the network was built.** The Japanese Knotweed clone that invaded Britain became commercially available in Europe only in 1848 and reached Kew in August 1850 (Bailey and Conolly 2000). The principal period of British main-line construction, and therefore of embankment building, was the 1830s and 1840s. A plant that arrived in 1850 cannot have been used to stabilise embankments that were already built. The 1825 introduction date sometimes produced in support of the railway story refers to a Chinese accession that played no part in the invasion.

- **There is no evidence of planting as a matter of policy.** We are not aware of any documentary evidence that Invasive Knotweeds were planted along railway embankments as company policy, and the contemporary horticultural record of railway bank planting does not name them. Engineers of the construction period did specify what was planted, Isambard Kingdom Brunel among them on the Great Western Railway, and what the record names for railway land is Hawthorn for the boundary hedge, with Scotch Broom and Common Gorse on the embankments.

- **Ornamental planting by individuals is documented, but it was individual.** Railway banks were planted ornamentally from the 1880s onwards, driven by station staff, adjacent landowners and voluntary associations rather than by the railway companies, and contemporaries described such efforts as unusual.

- **Garden escapes and cast-outs are an important historical source**, from the Victorian era to the present day, particularly near urban and historically industrialised areas (Conolly 1977).

- **Contemporary spread is a maintenance phenomenon.** Linear corridors are high-disturbance environments, and following establishment, further spread along them arises principally from necessary maintenance activity rather than from any historical decision to plant.

**The practical consequence:** where an Invasive Knotweed stand is found along a linear corridor, the available evidence does not support an assumption that it was deliberately planted there. The more probable explanation is introduction from an adjacent source, followed by redistribution along the corridor through disturbance.

## The argument that is not in the folklore

The earliest certain naturalisation in the British Isles is not a garden escape and not a railway bank. It is Storrie's 1886 record of the plant in abundance on cinder tips at Maesteg, Glamorgan, a town then at the heart of the Industrial Revolution, with the Llynfi and Maesteg iron works, their blast furnaces and extensive tips. Von Siebold was selling the plant for the consolidation of sandy hills and dunes, early German records point to its use as a soil stabiliser in Westphalia, and the step from sand dunes to slag heaps is not a large one.

The statement sets this out as the most plausible deliberate-planting route in the British record, and is explicit that it is a hypothesis rather than a documented fact: no document is known showing an ironmaster specifying Knotweed for a tip. Industrial tips frequently sat adjacent to the rail network, which is one way a stand can come to look railway-planted when no railway planted it.

## Three mechanisms, usually treated as one

Much of the confusion in practice comes from treating "spread along the corridor" as a single process. It is three, and they differ in what they move and how far:

| Mechanism | What it moves |
|---|---|
| Cutting and flail mowing | Aboveground stem material, in quantity, as small fragments (Colleran and Goodall 2015; Jones et al. 2020) |
| Groundworks and soil movement | Rhizome, the more reliable regenerative unit; historically the primary driver of spread between and beyond river catchments in the UK (Palmer 1990) |
| Air movement behind passing trains | Broken stem material, redistributed along the trackside |

A biosecurity regime written around one of these does not address the others.

## What the statement does not claim

Section 6 sets out the limitations in full, and they are not decorative. The horticultural record for railway-bank planting is incomplete and survives largely in periodicals and secondary accounts rather than in company records. The absence of Knotweed from that literature is qualified by the fact that the leading gardening authorities of the period, William Robinson and Gertrude Jekyll among them, were recommending Knotweed for rough and semi-wild ground in exactly the decades when railway banks were being planted up. Absence of documentation is not proof that planting did not occur.

The claims made are narrower than the ones a reader may expect: that the chronology of construction rules out Invasive Knotweed as a construction-period stabilisation species for most of the network, and that the available evidence does not support deliberate planting as a matter of policy thereafter. Neither amounts to asserting that no Invasive Knotweed was ever planted alongside a railway.

## Acknowledgement

This statement rests heavily on Ann Conolly's 1977 paper in *Watsonia*, the foundation of what is reliably known about the early British invasion history of these species, and the product of years of meticulous work through herbaria across the country. Her four decadal distribution maps still frame the subject. Much of the argument is simply her record, read carefully.
